Unhurried, but the new page is readable at 0.6s. The square is the same signal as the footer rows, so the system shares one mark.
  2. D · Timetable · 9.0. Content first: the old page fades, and the new page fills in block by block (heading, lead, image), each rising a little, like a timetable being filled in. No device of its own, which is its strength for the most common transition; pairs well with A.
  3. C · Signal sweep · 8.5. A hairline with the yellow square glides down the page and leaves the new page above it, like a departure board refreshing from the top. Calm and legible; a touch more "effect" than A.
  4. B · Board rows · 8.1. Reworked: the old page's rows lift off top to bottom on an overlapping, even stagger, each with a hairline that fades, while the new page settles underneath. Much smoother than round 1, still more motion than the everyday link needs.
  5. E · Departure · 7.9. The old page's blocks leave to the left, the new ones arrive from the right, the way the site moves (left to right). Clear direction, but sideways motion every time gets tiring.

When it runs

  • Every navigation that isn't to a service page (PT-01) or the quote page (PT-02): Home, About, Help, Privacy, Accessibility, the 404, and back and forward.
  • A and D can combine: the route line along the navbar while the new page fills in like D. That's my recommendation.
  • Reduced motion: a 150ms crossfade.

The system

  • Three types, picked from the link's address before the page leaves: service, quote, general (everything else, and back/forward).
  • One mechanism: native cross-document View Transitions plus about 1 KB of JS. No framework; Lenis and GSAP keep working.
  • The navbar is named once and never animates.
  • One easing family across all three: a long, soft ease-out for arrivals, an even curve for things that travel, nothing that bounces.
  • Browsers without View Transitions (Firefox today) navigate normally.
